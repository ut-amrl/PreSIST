Persistence definition:
An object persists only while it remains effectively unchanged in its current image location, pose, and articulated state. It stops persisting when its position, pose, or articulated state changes enough that it would no longer correspond to the same segmentation mask in a future image.

For articulated objects, movement of the articulated part counts as movement. Examples include a door opening or closing, a drawer sliding, a hand moving, a person shifting pose, a vehicle door changing angle, or a lid opening or closing.

For people, animals, and visible body parts, any meaningful body-part movement counts as movement of that object. A walking person, moving hand, reaching arm, or person manipulating an object should therefore have sub-second persistence.

Do not count tiny non-rigid environmental deformation, such as leaves fluttering, grass swaying, tiny fabric wrinkles, or minor shadow changes, unless the object as a whole changes location, pose, or articulated state.

Quantile definition:
- "p25_persistence_hours" is the time, in hours, by which there is approximately a 25% chance the object has changed location, pose, or articulated state enough to no longer match its current segmentation.
- "p75_persistence_hours" is the time, in hours, by which there is approximately a 75% chance the object has changed location, pose, or articulated state enough to no longer match its current segmentation.
- "p75_persistence_hours" must be greater than "p25_persistence_hours".
- The p25 and p75 values should be meaningfully separated so they represent uncertainty about persistence rather than two nearly identical point estimates.
- Unless the object’s persistence is extremely predictable, "p75_persistence_hours" should usually be at least 2x "p25_persistence_hours".
- For highly uncertain objects, "p75_persistence_hours" may be 5x to 100x "p25_persistence_hours" or more.
- For very predictable actively moving objects with sub-second persistence, "p75_persistence_hours" should still usually be at least 1.5x "p25_persistence_hours".

Guidelines:
- Consider the typical timescale for the object's likely persistence and then convert to hours.
- Do not infer imminent movement merely because an object is movable.
- Shorten estimates only when there is evidence of active motion, active use, nearby interaction, traffic flow, ongoing manipulation, or a process likely to move it soon.
- Lengthen estimates when the object is parked, stored, stacked, displayed, installed, part of furniture, part of infrastructure, rooted, or physically constrained, but only to the degree supported by the scene and object description.
- Objects that have some fixed components but can still move should not be treated as fully immovable. For example, the persistence estimate for a door concerns how long until it moves on its hinge.
- Structural or immovable objects such as walls, countertops, floors, ceilings, buildings, roads, curbs, and fixed poles should have very large persistence values, typically 10000 to 200000 hours.
- Consider whether something is truly fixed, such as a wall or light pole, or whether it can be moved but is not frequently moved, such as a poster, potted plant, large furniture, or display item. Objects that can be moved can have very long persistence times, but should usually not be as high as fully immovable entities.
- People, animals, and visible body parts should generally have very short persistence because any meaningful body-part movement, posture shift, head turn, limb movement, or step counts as movement.
- Vehicles in active traffic lanes or currently moving should have very short persistence, usually sub-second to a few seconds depending on whether they are visibly moving or temporarily stopped.
- Vehicles stopped at a light, in a queue, or at a crosswalk should usually have persistence of seconds to tens of seconds.
- Parked vehicles should be estimated based on the environment, activity level, nearby people, likely trip purpose, and whether there are cues of imminent use. For example, a parked car outside a grocery store may persist for a shorter time than one in a residential or low-turnover parking area, unless nearby interaction cues suggest otherwise.
- For objects that appear currently stationary but are capable of moving, estimate persistence based on typical patterns for the object, given the environment context. For example: a chair at an occupied desk may move sooner than a chair at an empty office desk at night; utensils at an occupied kitchen counter may move sooner than utensils in an unoccupied kitchen; a car at a grocery store may move sooner than a car at an office building.
- For retail merchandise, products on shelves, displayed items, stocked goods, and similar objects, estimate persistence based on shelf/display turnover, customer interaction, employee restocking, and whether the object appears actively handled or merely displayed. Items with no interaction cues often persist longer than loose objects in active use, but the exact timescale depends on the scene.
- For stacked, stored, shelved, boxed, binned, or grouped objects, consider both the storage context and the object’s position within the arrangement. Objects that are constrained, buried, lower in a stack, behind other items, or part of an organized display often persist longer than exposed, topmost, frontmost, or actively accessed objects. Do not assume all stacked or stored objects have the same timescale.
- For food, utensils, dishes, tools, personal items, and small loose objects in eating, cooking, cleaning, work, or other active-use contexts, estimate persistence based on whether the object is being handled, is within reach of an active person, appears recently used, is part of an ongoing task, or is unattended. Actively handled objects should usually have very short persistence; nearby but unhandled objects may persist longer depending on the activity and environment.
- Natural static objects such as trees, shrubs, grass, rocks, and landscape features should usually have long persistence. Ignore small wind-driven deformation unless the whole object changes location, pose, or segmentation-relevant state.
- Consider processes and activities suggested by the context that would influence movement of an object, for example:
  - Fork at an occupied table: consider how often someone would reach for the fork.
  - Used fork at an empty table at a restaurant: consider how quickly the table would be cleared.

  - Car parked outside a grocery store: consider how long someone would spend in a grocery store.
  - Small table at a cafe: consider how often it would be rearranged or moved for cleaning, compared with a large home dining room table or conference room table.
- "persistence_conf" should be high when the object state and context strongly determine the estimate, even if the estimate is very short. For example, a walking person can have high confidence with very short persistence.
- "persistence_conf" should be lower when the object identity, current state, interaction cues, or scene context are ambiguous.
- If uncertain, set "persistence_uncertainty" to "high" and use a wider p25–p75 interval.
