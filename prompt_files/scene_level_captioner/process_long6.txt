You are generating a scene-level caption to help estimate how long objects are likely to remain in their current location.

Write 3-8 sentences. Focus primarily on the processes in the environment that are likely to move, relocate, remove, clean, store, rearrange, alter, or otherwise change an object's physical state. Mention the likely type of place, whether it is indoor or outdoor, the approximate time of day if inferable, and the current and expected activity level.

For this task, meaningful movement includes full relocation as well as local movement, partial motion, changes in pose, orientation, placement, contact state, or articulated/separable-part state. Count brief lifting, repositioning, rotation, opening, closing, sliding, nudging, tilting, shifting, and articulation, such as movement of doors, drawers, lids, screens, or hinged parts. Do not consider purely repetitive oscillation or deformation-like motion, such as fluttering, swaying, rippling, shadows, or reflections, when it does not produce a lasting or discrete state change.

The main goal is to identify both current processes and likely future processes that govern object movement or state change in this environment.

First describe any process that appears to be happening right now or is visually cued as immediately about to happen. These immediate processes often determine the shortest persistence times. Frame these as processes or causal mechanisms, not just as lists of objects. Examples include:

- movement of people or other living creatures. People should be assumed to be moving to some degree unless there is evidence that they are completely still, such as sleeping or posed motionless.
- human handling or use: people reaching for, carrying, pushing, opening, loading, cleaning, eating, assembling, repairing, putting away, or otherwise manipulating objects
- temporary vehicle motion or stopping: vehicles stopped, queued, loading, unloading, parking, departing, or waiting at a stoplight, curb, crosswalk, loading zone, pickup area, or drive-through
- staging for near-term movement: carts, trays, tools, luggage, boxes, or other objects positioned for loading, unloading, checkout, transport, repair, cleaning, or storage
- mechanical or articulated motion: doors, drawers, lids, screens, gates, machinery, appliances, elevators, escalators, or equipment operating or changing state
- stocking, unpacking, repair, cleaning, or removal: items being stocked, unpacked, cleaned, repaired, assembled, discarded, cleared, or removed
- environmental displacement: loose lightweight objects such as leaves, paper, debris, bags, or trash being moved by wind, airflow, water, gravity, vibration, or contact

For each current process, briefly indicate what kinds of objects or object categories it could affect, the likely type of movement or state change, the approximate timescale for that movement — milliseconds-seconds, seconds-minutes, or minutes-hours — and whether the process is directly visible, visually implied, or merely plausible from scene type.

Then describe plausible future processes that may move, alter, clear, reset, replace, restock, store, remove, or reconfigure more stable objects later today, daily, weekly, seasonally, or only occasionally. Include infrequent processes when they are plausible for the setting, since even stable objects may eventually move because of them, but give more attention to the processes most likely to affect objects sooner. Clearly separate low-frequency background routines from currently active or imminent processes, and avoid implying that a routine is imminent unless there is direct visual evidence that it is underway.

For future processes, focus on the effective persistence timescale for affected objects: estimate how long from now until the object is likely to change state, including both the wait until the next likely process and the brief movement or handling time once it occurs. Do not describe an absent future interaction only by its eventual handling speed; for example, grocery items in an empty but open store may have a minutes-to-hours or hours-to-days persistence timescale even though pickup itself takes only seconds. Use seconds-to-minutes as the main persistence estimate only for objects already being handled, directly targeted, or tightly staged for imminent handling; otherwise, use the effective timescale from the current moment until the object is likely to change (i.e. combining the time until the process starts with change duration). For each future process, briefly indicate the object categories it may affect, how often it typically occurs, and the approximate effective persistence timescale given the current visual evidence.
Use scene type, time of day, occupancy, lighting, open/closed cues, weather, activity level, and whether the scene is unusually empty for its type to infer when agents or routine processes are likely to appear next. If agents are absent or other cues suggest immediate inactivity, estimate when agents are likely to return rather than assuming either immediate activity or indefinite inactivity. For example, a commuter parking lot in the morning implies many vehicles will move within hours, whereas a parking lot late at night likely won't have movement for several hours. Likewise, a kitchen in the afternoon will likely be occupied in a few hours for cooking dinner, while a kitchen at night likely won't have any processes until the morning.

Examples of useful future-process reasoning:

- in a bedroom, clothing or bags may move later today during normal use or cleanup, while furniture may remain until a less frequent deep-cleaning, reorganization, or move weeks to months later
- in an office, papers, mugs, or chairs may move during the workday or by the next workday, while desks, shelving, or equipment may move only during occasional reconfiguration, moves, repairs, or renovations over months to years
- in a parking lot, cars may leave or reposition within minutes to hours as part of shopping, commuting, pickup, delivery, or work-shift cycles, while cones, barriers, carts, or maintenance equipment may remain until collection, cleanup, or the next project phase
- in a restaurant, dishes and trays may be moved or lifted immediately during eating, cleared within minutes to hours when the party leaves, tables and movable seating may shift slightly during use, as customers pass by, or be reset between customers, while fixtures or decor change only during periodic cleaning or renovation
- in a living room, small objects may move during daily use, while furniture may move when hosting guests, or during occasional deep-cleaning or rearrangement
- in a store or grocery aisle, individual items may be nudged, picked up, inspected, purchased, fronted, or restocked. In a store with no visible customers, customer or staff activity is a plausible routine process, but the next interaction depends on time-to-agent-arrival, which can be informed by whether the location appears open, closed, busy, or unusually empty
- in outdoor scenes, wind or water may move loose lightweight objects, but branches swaying, flags flapping, signs swinging on fixed mounts, shadows, and reflections should usually be treated as deformation-like and not included in our motion prediction unless they change a relevant articulated state, placement, or configuration

When discussing persistence, distinguish among broad categories rather than giving a single estimate for everything.

Use the following rough guidance on time-scales:

- Treat visible people and animals as changing location or pose on milliseconds-to-seconds timescales unless they appear completely still, such as sleeping, posed, or otherwise motionless.
- Objects actively held, worn, carried, pushed, pulled, driven, or manipulated by people usually inherit very short timescales, often milliseconds-seconds to seconds-minutes.
- Objects temporarily set down but still associated with a nearby person or active process often move on seconds-minutes to minutes-hours timescales, depending on whether the person is actively using them, pausing briefly, eating, shopping, working, waiting, or preparing to leave. This includes objects whose type, placement, or context suggests temporary personal use, ownership, or an upcoming human-mediated process, such as bags, phones, coats, cups, plates, carts, luggage, tools, or personal items near occupied seats, tables, work areas, checkout areas, or transit areas. However, do not apply this shorter-timescale assumption to all objects merely because they are near people; nearby objects should only be treated this way when there is visual or contextual evidence linking them to the person or active process.
- Objects awaiting a visually implied next process should be described in terms of that process: identify the implied process, estimate the likely time until the next occurrence of that process and how long it takes for that process to affect objects once it occurs. Use that process’s movement timescale rather than a default range. Give a reasonable range when the timing is uncertain and note uncertainty.
- For most portable or movable objects not governed by an immediate visible process, persistence may plausibly fall anywhere from minutes to months.  Narrow the range based on the active or likely future processes that affect the object category, the scene evidence, and the expected timing of those processes. Estimate the effective timescale from the current moment until the object is likely to change, rather than only the duration of the eventual handling or movement. Objects affected mainly by periodic cleaning, maintenance, restocking, watering, reorganization, storage, seasonal changes, or occasional moves may persist for days-weeks, weeks-months, months-years, or longer depending on the frequency of those processes.
- Structural or installed elements usually persist for years or longer unless visible activity suggests repair, construction, removal, or reconfiguration.
Intermediate ranges such as “tens of minutes,” “several hours,” or “several days” are also acceptable when supported by visible activity, concrete staging cues, or known narrow timing patterns for the scene type.


When many similar or interchangeable objects are present, avoid assigning the fastest likely movement of any one item to every item in the group and instead represent the range of possible times for an arbitrary object in the group. For example, in a busy grocery aisle, a typical item may plausibly remain from minutes or hours to a day or a few days, depending on demand, product popularity, shelf area, stock rotation, and restocking, even though it is likely that at least one item is taken or moved within tens of minutes. In this case, the time scale should be "minutes up to potentially a few days".

If the relevant process is uncertain, explicitly describe the ambiguity and the different timescales it would imply. For example, a stack of boxes in a room might move within minutes if someone is currently unpacking, remain until later today if awaiting use, or persist for months until the next major reorganization. Use qualifiers such as “directly visible,” “visually implied,” “typical for this setting,” “if open/occupied,” or “speculative” to separate observation from scene-type routine assumptions. When timing is uncertain, avoid overly narrow estimates and use a broader range, or list multiple plausible timescale options (ex. "weeks to months or months to years").
